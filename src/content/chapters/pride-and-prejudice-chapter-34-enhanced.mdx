<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The opening passage establishes Elizabeth's emotional state before Darcy's arrival. By examining Jane's letters, Elizabeth reinforces her resentment toward Darcy, interpreting her sister's subdued tone as evidence of heartbreak caused by Darcy's interference. This preparatory scene is crucial for understanding Elizabeth's mindset—she enters the proposal scene already primed with anger and grief on behalf of her beloved sister. Austen skillfully uses the letter-reading device to create dramatic irony: the reader perceives Elizabeth's selective interpretation of evidence while simultaneously recognizing her genuine love for Jane. “Can you deny that you have done it?” she repeated.

With assumed tranquillity he then replied, “I have no wish of denying
that I did everything in my power to separate my friend from your
sister, or that I rejoice in my success. Towards _him_ I have been
kinder than towards myself.”

Elizabeth disdained the appearance of noticing this civil reflection,
but its meaning did not escape, nor was it likely to conciliate her.

“But it is not merely this affair,” she continued, “on which my dislike

<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The Wickham accusation and Darcy's devastating response regarding Elizabeth's 'connections' represent the absolute nadir of their confrontation. Wickham serves as Elizabeth's proxy for judging Darcy—his testimony has 'unfolded' Darcy's character in her mind. Darcy's contemptuous repetition of 'His misfortunes!' signals his suppressed fury at Wickham's continued influence on Elizabeth's opinion. The climax arrives with Darcy's counter-accusation about wounded pride, where he reveals that he believes Elizabeth's anger stems from his honesty about social inferiority rather than his actual offenses. His challenge—'Could you expect me to rejoice in the inferiority of your connections?'—exposes the authentic prejudice beneath his supposed love. Elizabeth's declaration that she was 'the last man in the world whom I could ever be prevailed on to marry' delivers the devastating coup de grâce that will haunt him into reformation. The chapter concludes with Elizabeth's private agony, revealing that her triumph of principle has not spared her emotional turmoil.
