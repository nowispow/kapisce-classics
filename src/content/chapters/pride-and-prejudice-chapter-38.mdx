On Saturday morning Elizabeth and Mr. Collins met for breakfast a few
minutes before the others appeared; and he took the opportunity of
paying the parting civilities which he deemed indispensably necessary.

{/* metadata:quote_001:start */}
<Callout title="Memorable Quote" variant="note">“I know not, Miss Elizabeth,” said he, “whether Mrs. Collins has yet expressed her sense of your kindness in coming to us; but I am very certain you will not leave the house without receiving her thanks for it.</Callout>
{/* metadata:quote_001:end */}

The favour of your company has been much felt, I assure you. We know
how little there is to tempt anyone to our humble abode.

{/* metadata:quote_002:start */}
<Callout title="Memorable Quote" variant="note">Our plain manner of living, our small rooms, and few domestics, and the little we see of the world, must make Hunsford extremely dull to a young lady like yourself; but I hope you will believe us grateful for the condescension, and that we have done everything in our power to prevent you spending your time unpleasantly.”</Callout>
{/* metadata:quote_002:end */}

{/* metadata:analysis_001:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
Mr. Collins's farewell speech exemplifies his characteristic blend of self-deprecation and self-congratulation. While ostensibly apologizing for the humble conditions at Hunsford, he simultaneously emphasizes the superior social connections through Lady Catherine, revealing his inability to distinguish genuine hospitality from social climbing. His mention of 'condescension' betrays his class consciousness and desire to position himself advantageously, even in the act of saying goodbye.
</Callout>

Elizabeth was eager with her thanks and assurances of happiness. She had
spent six weeks with great enjoyment; and the pleasure of being with
Charlotte, and the kind attention she had received, must make _her_ feel
the obliged. Mr. Collins was gratified; and with a more smiling
solemnity replied,--

{/* metadata:quote_003:start */}
<Callout title="Memorable Quote" variant="note">“It gives me the greatest pleasure to hear that you have passed your time not disagreeably.</Callout>
{/* metadata:quote_003:end */}
{/* metadata:analysis_002:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The phrase 'smiling solemnity' used to describe Mr. Collins's reply is a masterful oxymoron capturing the absurdity of his character. Austen uses this paradoxical expression to highlight how Collins attempts to balance his perpetual need for gravity with his desire to appear pleased, resulting in an awkward hybrid that Elizabeth must navigate with careful diplomacy. The phrase encapsulates in two words what the entire chapter dramatizes at length: the comic gap between Collins's self-image and the reality others perceive.
</Callout>
{/* metadata:analysis_002:end */}

We have certainly done our best; and most
fortunately having it in our power to introduce you to very superior
society, and from our connection with Rosings, the frequent means of
varying the humble home scene, I think we may flatter ourselves that
your Hunsford visit cannot have been entirely irksome. Our situation
with regard to Lady Catherine’s family is, indeed, the sort of
extraordinary advantage and blessing which few can boast. You see on
what a footing we are. You see how continually we are engaged there. In
truth, I must acknowledge, that, with all the disadvantages of this
humble parsonage, I should not think anyone abiding in it an object of
compassion, while they are sharers of our intimacy at Rosings.”

{/* metadata:analysis_003:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
Collins's boast of 'superior society' and 'extraordinary advantage and blessing' in connection with Rosings reveals the central irony of the novel: genuine happiness and social elevation are rarely synonymous. His obliviousness to the actual quality of these social interactions--marked by Lady Catherine's tyranny and his own sycophancy--underscores Austen's critique of social hierarchies and false values. That he presents proximity to Rosings as compensation for every domestic deficiency exposes how thoroughly he has confused deference with dignity.
</Callout>
{/* metadata:analysis_003:end */}

“You may, in fact, carry a very favourable report of us into
Hertfordshire, my dear cousin. I flatter myself, at least, that you will
be able to do so. Lady Catherine’s great attentions to Mrs. Collins you
have been a daily witness of; and altogether I trust it does not appear
that your friend has drawn an unfortunate--but on this point it will be
as well to be silent. Only let me assure you, my dear Miss Elizabeth,
that I can from my heart most cordially wish you equal felicity in
marriage.

{/* metadata:quote_005:start */}
<Callout title="Memorable Quote" variant="note">My dear Charlotte and I have but one mind and one way of thinking. There is in everything a most remarkable resemblance of character and ideas between us. We seem to have been designed for each other.”</Callout>
{/* metadata:quote_005:end */}

Elizabeth could safely say that it was a great happiness where that was
the case, and with equal sincerity could add, that she firmly believed
and rejoiced in his domestic comforts. She was not sorry, however, to
have the recital of them interrupted by the entrance of the lady from
whom they sprang.

{/* metadata:quote_006:start */}
<Callout title="Memorable Quote" variant="note">Poor Charlotte! it was melancholy to leave her to such society! But she had chosen it with her eyes open; and though evidently regretting that her visitors were to go, she did not seem to ask for compassion.</Callout>
{/* metadata:quote_006:end */}

Her home and her housekeeping, her parish and her poultry,
and all their dependent concerns, had not yet lost their charms.

{/* metadata:analysis_005:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
Austen's authorial aside 'Poor Charlotte! it was melancholy to leave her to such society!' marks a rare direct intervention revealing Elizabeth's true feelings. This moment of frank sympathy contrasts sharply with the constrained dialogue, offering readers privileged insight into Elizabeth's judgment of Charlotte's marriage while acknowledging Charlotte's agency in choosing her fate. The observation that Charlotte 'did not seem to ask for compassion' further complicates the reader's response, suggesting that Charlotte's contentment, however limited, is genuine. Her home and housekeeping, parish and poultry, retain their hold on her, and Austen renders this without condescension. The passage thus holds two truths simultaneously: Elizabeth's pity is real, and Charlotte's satisfaction is real.
</Callout>
{/* metadata:analysis_005:end */}

At length the chaise arrived, the trunks were fastened on, the parcels
placed within, and it was pronounced to be ready. After an affectionate
parting between the friends, Elizabeth was attended to the carriage by
Mr. Collins; and as they walked down the garden, he was commissioning
her with his best respects to all her family, not forgetting his thanks
for the kindness he had received at Longbourn in the winter, and his
compliments to Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner, though unknown. He then handed
her in, Maria followed, and the door was on the point of being closed,
when he suddenly reminded them, with some consternation, that they had
hitherto forgotten to leave any message for the ladies of Rosings.

“But,” he added, “you will of course wish to have your humble respects
delivered to them, with your grateful thanks for their kindness to you
while you have been here.”

Elizabeth made no objection: the door was then allowed to be shut, and
the carriage drove off.

{/* metadata:analysis_006:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The comedy of Mr. Collins's sudden recollection that they had forgotten to leave messages for the ladies of Rosings perfectly captures his social anxiety and obsession with proper forms. His 'consternation' at this oversight reveals how thoroughly he has internalized the rituals of deference to Lady Catherine's family, treating social obligation with the gravity of religious duty. That this moment of panic occurs at the very threshold of departure--door nearly shut, carriage ready--underscores how completely the performance of deference governs his every action.
</Callout>
{/* metadata:analysis_006:end */}

“Good gracious!” cried Maria, after a few minutes’ silence, “it seems
but a day or two since we first came! and yet how many things have
happened!”

“A great many indeed,” said her companion, with a sigh.

“We have dined nine times at Rosings, besides drinking tea there twice!
How much I shall have to tell!”

{/* metadata:quote_007:start */}
<Callout title="Memorable Quote" variant="note">Elizabeth privately added, “And how much I shall have to conceal!”</Callout>
{/* metadata:quote_007:end */}

{/* metadata:analysis_007:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
Elizabeth's private reflection 'And how much I shall have to conceal!' following Maria's innocent tally of social events creates dramatic irony and underscores the chapter's tension between surface appearances and hidden truths. This contrast between Maria's superficial accounting and Elizabeth's weighty secrets encapsulates the novel's exploration of how social events conceal rather than reveal true significance. The juxtaposition of Maria's nine dinners at Rosings against Elizabeth's unspoken knowledge of Darcy's proposal and Wickham's villainy measures the vast distance between social surface and psychological depth. The chapter closes not on departure but on interiority, reminding the reader that the most consequential events of the visit have left no visible trace.
</Callout>

Their journey was performed without much conversation, or any alarm; and
within four hours of their leaving Hunsford they reached Mr. Gardiner’s
house, where they were to remain a few days.

Jane looked well, and Elizabeth had little opportunity of studying her
spirits, amidst the various engagements which the kindness of her aunt
had reserved for them. But Jane was to go home with her, and at
Longbourn there would be leisure enough for observation.

It was not without an effort, meanwhile, that she could wait even for
Longbourn, before she told her sister of Mr. Darcy’s proposals. To know
that she had the power of revealing what would so exceedingly astonish
Jane, and must, at the same time, so highly gratify whatever of her own
vanity she had not yet been able to reason away, was such a temptation
to openness as nothing could have conquered, but the state of indecision
in which she remained as to the extent of what she should communicate,
and her fear, if she once entered on the subject, of being hurried into
repeating something of Bingley, which might only grieve her sister
further.
