Elizabeth had the satisfaction of receiving an answer to her letter as
soon as she possibly could. She was no sooner in possession of it, than
hurrying into the little copse, where she was least likely to be
interrupted, she sat down on one of the benches, and prepared to be
happy; for the length of the letter convinced her that it did not
contain a denial.

The chapter's tonal shift from revelation to confrontation occurs when Wickham interrupts Elizabeth's solitary reflection. Their dialogue functions as a dramatic test of Elizabeth's newfound knowledge and her strategic restraint. Wickham's falseness becomes newly visible—his pretended envy of her visit to Pemberley, his calculated reference to the Kympton living, and his dissembling about his relationship with Darcy all serve to expose his character through contrast with Darcy's authentic nobility, and Elizabeth's brisk pace away from him is rendered with quiet irony: 'she had walked fast to get rid of him.'
