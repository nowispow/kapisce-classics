 Collins made his
declaration in form. Having resolved to do it without loss of time, as
his leave of absence extended only to the following Saturday, and having
no feelings of diffidence to make it distressing to himself even at the
moment, he set about it in a very orderly manner, with all the
observances which he supposed a regular part of the business.

{/* metadata:a1:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
Mr. Collins' proposal exemplifies Austen's mastery of ironic characterization and satirical prose. His methodical enumeration of reasons for marrying—setting an example for his parish, personal happiness, and Lady Catherine's recommendation—reveals not genuine affection but self-interest and social obligation. The absurdity of his 'violence of my affection' declaration, preceded by calculating paragraphs about entailment and financial practicalities, creates comedic tension that exposes the mercenary nature of many Regency marriages. Austen contrasts Collins' clerical pomposity with Elizabeth's quick wit and moral integrity throughout the scene.
</Callout>

Before Elizabeth had time for anything but a blush of surprise, Mrs.
Bennet instantly answered,--

“Oh dear! Yes, certainly. I am sure Lizzy will be very happy--I am sure
she can have no objection. Come, Kitty, I want you upstairs.” And
gathering her work together, she was hastening away, when Elizabeth
called out,--

“Dear ma’am, do not go. I beg you will not go. Mr. Collins must excuse
me. He can have nothing to say to me that anybody need not hear. I am
going away myself.”

“No, no, nonsense, Lizzy. I desire you will stay where you are.” And
upon Elizabeth’s seeming really, with vexed and embarrassed looks, about
to escape, she added, “Lizzy, I _insist_ upon your staying and hearing
Mr. Collins.”

Elizabeth would not oppose such an injunction; and a moment’s
consideration making her also sensible that it would be wisest to get it
over as soon and as quietly as possible, she sat down again, and tried
to conceal, by incessant employment, the feelings which were divided
between distress and diversion.

<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The power dynamics between mother and daughter drive the scene's opening drama. Mrs. Bennet's eagerness to secure Elizabeth's compliance—her immediate agreement, her assertion that Lizzy 'can have no objection,' and her maternal insistence—reveals a mother's desperate socio-economic anxiety. The entailment that threatens Longbourn transforms maternal affection into strategic maneuvering. Elizabeth's vexed embarrassment and attempted escape are overridden by her mother's injunction, yet her eventual acquiescence reflects pragmatic wisdom: a moment's consideration making her also sensible that it would be wisest to get it over as soon and as quietly as possible.

<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The theme of entailment pervades this chapter as both plot device and social critique. Mr. Collins frames his choice of Longbourn daughters as benevolent mitigation of their eventual loss, yet his phrasing—'when the melancholy event takes place'—betrays eager anticipation masked as concern. This economic reality, which makes Mrs. Bennet so desperate and Elizabeth's refusal so risky, illuminates how property law shaped women's destinies. Elizabeth's small portion of one thousand pounds in the 4 per cents., which will not be yours till after your mother's decease, becomes both weapon and vulnerability—Collins' weapon in attempted persuasion, and the economic reason women could not afford principled refusal.
</Callout>

{/* metadata:a4:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The rhetoric of Mr. Collins' proposal deserves careful analysis as a specimen of Austen's comic genius. His declaration unravels through careful logical exposition: first, why clergy should marry; second, his advantageous situation; third, his connection to Longbourn's entailment. The proposal's climax—his promise of silence about her small fortune—intended as generous forbearance, ironically underscores his complete misunderstanding of romantic language. His insistence that it is usual with young ladies to reject the addresses of the man whom they secretly mean to accept reveals the dangerous assumptions about women that preserved patriarchal structures.
</Callout>

<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
Lady Catherine de Bourgh casts a long shadow over this proposal, illustrating how aristocratic influence penetrates every level of provincial society. Mr. Collins presents her patronage as a marital asset, describing her unsolicited advice with reverential detail—the quadrille game, Mrs. Jenkinson arranging the footstool, the verbatim quotation of her imperious commands. This absurd deference to rank exposes how the gentry-class clergy internalized aristocratic values. Elizabeth's response—that Lady Catherine would find her in every respect ill qualified for the situation—demonstrates her recognition that Collins' criteria and her own values are fundamentally incompatible.
</Callout>

“Were it certain that Lady Catherine would think so,” said Mr. Collins,
very gravely--“but I cannot imagine that her Ladyship would at all
disapprove of you. And you may be certain that when I have the honour of
seeing her again I shall speak in the highest terms of your modesty,
economy, and other amiable qualifications.”

“Indeed, Mr. Collins, all praise of me will be unnecessary. You must
give me leave to judge for myself, and pay me the compliment of
believing what I say. I wish you very happy and very rich, and by
refusing your hand, do all in my power to prevent your being otherwise.
In making me the offer, you must have satisfied the delicacy of your
feelings with regard to my family, and may take possession of Longbourn
estate whenever it falls, without any self-reproach. This matter may be
considered, therefore, as finally settled.” And rising as she thus
spoke, she would have quitted the room, had not Mr. Collins thus
addressed her,--

“When I do myself the honour of speaking to you next on the subject, I
shall hope to receive a more favourable answer than you have now given
me; though I am far from accusing you of cruelty at present, because I
know it to be the established custom of your sex to reject a man on the
first application, and, perhaps, you have even now said as much to
encourage my suit as would be consistent with the true delicacy of the
female character.”

“Really, Mr. Collins,” cried Elizabeth, with some warmth, “you puzzle me
exceedingly. If what I have hitherto said can appear to you in the form
of encouragement, I know not how to express my refusal in such a way as
may convince you of its being one.”

“You must give me leave to flatter myself, my dear cousin, that your
refusal of my addresses are merely words of course. My reasons for
believing it are briefly these:--It does not appear to me that my hand
is unworthy of your acceptance, or that the establishment I can offer
would be any other than highly desirable. My situation in life, my
connections with the family of De Bourgh, and my relationship to your
own, are circumstances highly in my favour; and you should take it into
further consideration that, in spite of your manifold attractions, it is
by no means certain that another offer of marriage may ever be made you.
Your portion is unhappily so small, that it will in all likelihood undo
the effects of your loveliness and amiable qualifications. As I must,
therefore, conclude that you are not serious in your rejection of me, I
shall choose to attribute it to your wish of increasing my love by
suspense, according to the usual practice of elegant females.”

“I do assure you, sir, that I have no pretensions whatever to that kind
of elegance which consists in tormenting a respectable man. I would
rather be paid the compliment of being believed sincere. I thank you
again and again for the honour you have done me in your proposals, but
to accept them is absolutely impossible. My feelings in every respect
forbid it. Can I speak plainer?

{/* metadata:a5:start */}
<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
Elizabeth's refusal tactics evolve throughout the exchange, showcasing her rhetorical skill under pressure. Initial directness ('it is impossible for me to do otherwise than decline them') yields to elaborate self-explanation ('I am the last woman in the world who would make you so') and finally philosophical distinction ('as a rational creature speaking the truth from her heart'). Her repeated insistence on plain speaking—'Can I speak plainer?'—highlights the absurdity that her clarity requires such labor. The contrast between Collins' elaborate circumlocutions and Elizabeth's demand for transparent communication embodies Enlightenment values of reason over social pretense.
</Callout>

“You are uniformly charming!” cried he, with an air of awkward
gallantry; “and I am persuaded that, when sanctioned by the express
authority of both your excellent parents, my proposals will not fail of
being acceptable.”

To such perseverance in wilful self-deception Elizabeth would make no
reply, and immediately and in silence withdrew; determined, that if he
persisted in considering her repeated refusals as flattering
encouragement, to apply to her father, whose negative might be uttered
in such a manner as must be decisive, and whose behaviour at least could
not be mistaken for the affectation and coquetry of an elegant female.

<Callout title="Analysis" variant="explanation">
The chapter's conclusion establishes Elizabeth's father as her necessary protector against social coercion. Her determination to apply to her father, whose negative might be uttered in such a manner as must be decisive, reveals Austen's exploration of father-daughter bonds as crucial bulwarks against improper matches. The paternal veto represents one of few avenues of female agency available to women in Austen's world. Mr. Bennet's anticipated behaviour is explicitly contrasted with the affectation and coquetry of an elegant female falsely attributed to Elizabeth, suggesting that authentic masculine authority can recognize genuine feminine rationality where deluded suitors see only strategic flirtation.
